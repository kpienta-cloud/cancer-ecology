In memoriam

The Chief.

Donald S. Coffey, Ph.D. · 1932–2017 · Father of prostate cancer research at Johns Hopkins.

Biography

A scientist, a teacher, a chief.

Donald S. Coffey arrived at Johns Hopkins in 1960 with a chemistry degree, a slide rule, and an unusual capacity for asking simple questions about complicated systems. Over the next five decades he built the scientific program that became the modern Brady Urological Institute and trained generations of urologic oncologists who would carry his thinking to every corner of the field.

His own science ranged across nuclear matrix biology, prostate-cancer biochemistry, and the architecture of the cell — but his deeper contribution was a way of seeing. He treated cancer as a problem of organization, not of any one molecule. He insisted that the right scale of question was the whole cell, the whole organ, the whole patient. He was speaking the language of systems biology twenty years before the field had a name.

To his trainees he was simply The Chief. He taught by Socratic interrogation in his office, by long telephone calls late into the night, and by an annual Saint Patrick’s Day Lecture in which he would walk the entire department through the questions he was still asking after fifty years of looking.

Don Coffey died in 2017. The questions he was asking did not.

A tradition

The Saint Patrick’s Day Lecture.

For more than thirty years, every March 17th, Don Coffey delivered a lecture in the Brady to the assembled department — faculty, fellows, residents, students, and anyone who could fit in the room. The lectures had no fixed topic. They were a tour through whatever he was thinking about that year: a new paper that had surprised him, an old experiment he had never quite finished, a question that had been gnawing at him since 1971.

The lectures were not a recitation of results. They were an exercise in how to ask. He would put a question on the board, slow down, and walk through what it would take to answer it — what data, what instruments, what cohorts, what assumptions. By the end the room would have learned more about how to think than about whatever the year’s subject had been.

The Saint Patrick’s Day Lecture continues today in the Brady. The Cancer Ecology Center carries its spirit forward.

A teaching

Don Coffey’s Final Exam.

Late in his career, Coffey distilled what he had learned into a set of principles he asked every trainee to keep on their desk. The full list is below.

  1. If this is true, what does it imply?

    The first principle. Every observation, every result, every assumption opens a chain of consequences. Follow the chain.

  2. What scale is the question at?

    Cells, organs, organisms, populations. Most arguments in cancer biology are confusions of scale. Name the scale before you reason.

  3. What would falsify it?

    A hypothesis that cannot be wrong is not a hypothesis. Write down the experiment that would kill your idea, then go run it.

  4. Who has done this before?

    Read the old papers. Most of what feels new has been asked, often beautifully, by someone you have never heard of. Find them.

  5. What is the simplest model that fits?

    Complicated models almost always fit. Simple ones tell you something.

  6. Where does the energy come from?

    Biology runs on thermodynamics. If your model violates the second law, fix the model.

  7. What is the patient’s question?

    If the science cannot eventually be told to a patient in plain language, you are not done.

A manifesto

Creed of the Brady Long Rifles.

We are scientists first and last. We do not flinch from a hard question. We honour the patient by telling the truth.— Coffey’s manifesto for his lab, circa 1976

The Long Rifles — Coffey’s name for the lab — were expected to be marksmen of the question. The work was hard, the standards were absolute, and the loyalty was lifelong. The Cancer Ecology Center is one branch of that lineage.

The lineage

The Coffey Professorship.

In 2008 the Brady Urological Institute endowed the Donald S. Coffey Professorship in Urology through gifts from former trainees, patients, and friends of the laboratory. The chair was created so that Don Coffey’s way of asking questions would have a permanent home at Hopkins.

The chair is currently held by Kenneth J. Pienta, MD, Don Coffey’s student and longtime colleague. The Cancer Ecology Center is Coffey’s framework one generation later — eco-evolutionary in its language, but Coffey’s in its method.

The Cancer Ecology Center is one answer to questions Don Coffey was asking forty years ago.
