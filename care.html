Every research program in the Cancer Ecology Center is built around a single question: what would actually help the patient sitting in front of us?

A message from Dr. Pienta

Thank you, guys.

Humanity has been given a great gift: by comprehending our own mortality, we are constantly forced to reconsider the value of our lives.

I am taking this opportunity to write a thank you to my patients — most of them men over the age of 50 — who have taught me what age and mortality has taught them: valuing every day, and putting value back in for the good of others.

One of the best parts about being a physician is that I get to constantly learn from my patients. I think that most patients, when they come to the doctor, do not realize that they are giving their physician a gift — the humbling gift of being asked to join the intimate circle of their lives. This is especially true for oncologists, as we take a journey with our patients and their loved ones that often ends in their deaths.

Cancer is a scary thing — for the person who has it, for their families, and for their friends. Incurable cancer, like metastatic prostate cancer, is even scarier. Rarely do I see it paralyze my men. There is anger and grief, but what amazes me is the indomitable human spirit that says I accept that I have this and let's see what we can do about it. And almost everyone says: what can I do to help someone else who is going to be diagnosed with this? That is the value proposition. My patients have taught me that in the face of serious illness, they think of themselves last and others first. Not a bad way to approach life.

In sharing their journey in the last part of their life, I have learned to take the extra few minutes during each visit not only to learn who they are now, but who they have been. What they have seen, what they have experienced, what was and is of value to them. It has been a constant source of amazement to me that no matter what job someone had, what schooling, where they lived or whom they met, every single person has spent time trying to leave the world a better place. Quietly. No horn blowing. Just day-to-day living a life that makes a difference to those around them.

My guys with cancer seem to have grace. They value every day as a gift. They want to make sure everyone around them is taken care of. They want to help others if they can. They don't have time to look at the world as a negative place. They have taught me not to waste time on things that don't matter. To spend every day making a difference.

My patients, my men, have taught me to value life, and have propelled me to live a mission-driven life of meaning. That is why I wake up every morning fighting cancer.

Thanks guys,
Ken

Prostate Cancer Precision Medicine Center of Excellence

A clinical-research integration that brings genomic profiling, novel therapeutics, and clinical trial access together at the point of care for men with advanced prostate cancer. The Brady's flagship effort in molecularly-guided treatment.

PSMA Imaging

Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) PET imaging — typically with F-18-labeled tracers — detects cancer foci that conventional imaging misses, especially in lymph nodes and small distant deposits. The clinical implication is enormous: staging that is several orders of magnitude more accurate changes who is offered curative therapy, who is offered focal treatment, and who needs systemic management.

Hopkins InHealth

A Johns Hopkins institution-wide effort to build a continuous clinical observatory — linking EHRs, imaging, and biospecimens longitudinally for every consenting patient. InHealth is the partner that makes ecological digital-twin work operational rather than theoretical, and is a cross-link to our Treatment layer.

Active Surveillance in Prostate Cancer

For carefully selected men with low-risk localized disease, periodic monitoring is often preferable to immediate intervention. Active surveillance protocols at the Brady are calibrated to catch transitions to clinically meaningful disease early without subjecting patients to the harms of unnecessary treatment.

Prostate Cancer Staging

Accurate staging combines imaging (including PSMA-PET), pathology, and molecular signatures into a single coherent picture. The right treatment decision starts here.

Oligometastatic Cancer Overview & Total Eradication Treatment (TET) Trials

When metastatic disease is limited to a small number of sites, an aggressive multimodal approach — combining systemic therapy with local treatment of every visible deposit — may produce durable disease control. Total Eradication Treatment (TET) trials are testing this hypothesis prospectively.

Peripheral Androgen Blockade for Recurrent PCA

For biochemical recurrence after definitive local therapy, peripheral androgen blockade offers a strategy aimed at controlling disease while preserving aspects of quality of life that traditional androgen deprivation can affect.

Castrate Resistant Prostate Cancer

The clinical setting where the framework matters most. Castrate-resistant disease is where adaptive therapy, combination strategies, and evolutionary game-theoretic insights are being translated into trials and bedside decisions — directly connected to the Detection layer.

Current Clinical Trials

The Brady runs a portfolio of trials spanning early disease through castrate-resistant settings. Patients and referring physicians are welcome to inquire about open studies and eligibility.
