Pillar 01 · Risk

Genes & Exposures.

What determines who gets cancer in the first place? Combinatorial flux through tissue-specific networks, modulated by inherited variants, clonal mosaicism, and lifetime exposure history. Not one chemical. Not one gene.

§ 01

Why exposure is the right starting point.

Cancer-causing chemicals do not act in isolation. They pass through bioactivation and detoxification networks — CYP450s, UGTs, GSTs, NATs, SULTs, ALDHs — that are tissue-specific, genetically polymorphic, ancestry-stratified, and dose-non-linear. The same parent compound becomes a high-affinity DNA adduct in one tissue and an excreted glucuronide in another.

The conventional epidemiology of single chemicals against single endpoints flattens this. It treats the exposome as a checkbox, the genotype as a covariate, and the network as a black box. The combinatorics it misses are not small. In our ExposoGraph 2.0 simulations, the cancer risk score varies by up to 47-fold across plausible genotype configurations exposed to identical chemical mixtures.

Risk is the first pillar because every downstream pillar — Detection and Treatment — is conditioned on what arrived at the cell. If the arriving signal is mis-described, everything built on top of it is mis-described.

§ 02

The GeoToxGraph contrast move.

A handful of soil and sediment microbes — most prominently Geobacter — respire on chemicals that give humans cancer. They use aromatic hydrocarbons and chlorinated solvents as electron donors and acceptors. We do not.

Why? Pathway loss. Across deep evolutionary time, the human lineage lost detoxification machinery that the microbial world retained. We kept the partial pathways that produce reactive intermediates and lost the terminal pathways that disposed of them. The remainder is a network with leaks: parent compounds enter, intermediates accumulate, adducts form.

We call the asymmetry between input toxicity and microbial benefit a toxic inversion. GeoToxGraph maps where these inversions occur across hundreds of putative human carcinogens, and it identifies the molecular handful for which loss-of-function in the host explains the disease epidemiology better than any direct mechanism does.

§ 03

Inherited variants and clonal mosaicism.

Risk is not exhausted by exposure flux. Layered on top of it are inherited variants in the bioactivation/detox enzymes themselves — CYP1A1*2A, CYP2D6 null alleles, NAT2 slow acetylators, GSTM1/T1 deletions, SULT1A1*2, UGT1A1*28, ALDH2*2 — whose ancestry-stratified distributions produce ancestry-specific risk architectures. The same chemical exposure does not produce the same risk in two genetic backgrounds.

Layered on top of that is clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP): somatically acquired mutations (DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, JAK2) that expand clonally in apparently healthy blood and shift the risk of subsequent malignancy. CHIP is a moving target: a somatic modifier that drifts over a lifetime and reads forward into solid-tumor incidence and treatment-emergent cancers. It belongs in the Risk pillar because it is part of who you are before any tumor has emerged.

Together, exposure flux, inherited variants, and clonal mosaicism define the individual-level risk landscape that the BGES cohort, the CarcinoGenomics R01, the All of Us adapter, and international partnerships are built to characterize.

Adjacent research programs

The Cancer Swamp & Cancer as a Social Dysfunction.

The Cancer Swamp. Long before a tumor is detectable, a host tissue can be primed by years of chronic exposure into a state we call the swamp — a chronically inflamed, metabolically dysregulated, microbiome-shifted, oxidatively stressed niche in which the probability of any single transformed cell finding hospitable ground rises sharply. The swamp is the right object for the Risk pillar: it is the host-tissue context that makes cancer possible.

Cancer as a Social Dysfunction. Cancer incidence is not evenly distributed across zip codes, occupations, or histories. Risk is therefore inseparable from social determinants — diet access, occupational chemistry, housing-stock toxicants, healthcare access for early detection. We treat these not as confounders but as first-class inputs to the risk model.

Both threads converge on the same operational claim: combinatorial exposure plus genetic background plus social geography, not single mutagens, is what determines who develops cancer.

Open questions

What we are working on now.

  1. How tissue-specific is the bioactivation flux for the top 100 known IARC Group 1 carcinogens, and where does tissue specificity dominate genotype variation?
  2. For which carcinogens is the dominant story microbial pathway loss rather than direct adduct formation? GeoToxGraph is built to answer this.
  3. What does the Hopkins BGES cohort look like at n = 1,000 with full exposure histories and full pharmacogenomic profiles — and how does ancestry-stratified pharmacogenomics reshape population risk maps?
  4. How much of an individual's lifetime cancer risk is explained by combinatorial exposure flux, versus inherited variants, versus clonal hematopoiesis (CHIP)?
  5. How does the exposure-flux vector feed forward into the digital twin as the E sub-state in CEDT?
