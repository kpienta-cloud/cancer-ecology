Layer 01 · Origin

The Exposure Layer.

Cancer is not "caused by carcinogens" in a one-chemical, one-gene sense. It emerges from the combinatorial flux through tissue-specific bioactivation networks, modulated by inherited variants, clonal mosaicism, and lifetime exposure history.

§ 01

Why exposure is the right starting point.

Cancer-causing chemicals do not act in isolation. They pass through bioactivation and detoxification networks — CYP450s, UGTs, GSTs, NATs, SULTs — that are tissue-specific, genetically polymorphic, and dose-non-linear. The same parent compound becomes a high-affinity DNA adduct in one tissue and an excreted glucuronide in another.

The conventional epidemiology of single chemicals against single endpoints flattens this. It treats the exposome as a checkbox, the genotype as a covariate, and the network as a black box. The combinatorics it misses are not small. In our ExposoGraph 2.0 simulations, the cancer risk score varies by up to 47-fold across plausible genotype configurations exposed to identical chemical mixtures.

This is the right starting point because every downstream layer — tumor behavior, twin forecasting, clinical decision — is conditioned on what arrived at the cell. If the arriving signal is mis-described, everything built on top of it is mis-described.

§ 02

The GeoToxGraph contrast move.

A handful of soil and sediment microbes — most prominently Geobacter — respire on chemicals that give humans cancer. They use aromatic hydrocarbons and chlorinated solvents as electron donors and acceptors. We do not.

Why? Pathway loss. Across deep evolutionary time, the human lineage lost detoxification machinery that the microbial world retained. We kept the partial pathways that produce reactive intermediates and lost the terminal pathways that disposed of them. The remainder is a network with leaks: parent compounds enter, intermediates accumulate, adducts form.

We call the asymmetry between input toxicity and microbial benefit a toxic inversion. GeoToxGraph maps where these inversions occur across hundreds of putative human carcinogens, and it identifies the molecular handful for which loss-of-function in the host explains the disease epidemiology better than any direct mechanism does.

§ 03

The IsoMCED readout.

A 5 cm hair strand contains a six-month record of metabolic state, structured in keratin. The stable-isotope ratios of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur in keratin track diet, protein turnover, oxidative load, and — we propose — incipient malignancy.

The IsoMCED hypothesis is that early cancers shift host metabolism in the direction of Warburg-style anaplerosis, altered nitrogen cycling, and dysregulated sulfur amino acid metabolism, and that those shifts leave a measurable triplet signature in hair keratin long before a tumor produces a ctDNA-detectable signal.

The economics are the second story: under twenty dollars per sample at population scale. If the signature holds, IsoMCED is the cheapest population-scale cancer screen ever proposed.

Adjacent research programs

The Cancer Swamp & Cancer as a Social Dysfunction.

The Cancer Swamp. Long before a tumor is detectable, a host tissue can be primed by years of chronic exposure into a state we call the swamp — a chronically inflamed, metabolically dysregulated, microbiome-shifted, oxidatively stressed niche in which the probability of any single transformed cell finding hospitable ground rises sharply. Mapping the swamp is a prerequisite to risk prediction that goes beyond germline genetics.

Cancer as a Social Dysfunction. Cancer incidence is not evenly distributed across zip codes, occupations, or histories. The exposure layer is therefore inseparable from social determinants — diet access, occupational chemistry, housing-stock toxicants, healthcare access for early detection. We treat these not as confounders but as first-class inputs to the exposure model.

Both threads converge on the same operational claim: combinatorial exposure plus social geography, not single mutagens, is what determines who develops cancer.

Open questions

What we are working on now.

  1. How tissue-specific is the bioactivation flux for the top 100 known IARC Group 1 carcinogens, and where does the tissue specificity dominate genotype variation?
  2. For which carcinogens is the dominant story microbial pathway loss rather than direct adduct formation? GeoToxGraph is built to answer this.
  3. Can the isotope triplet (δ¹³C / δ¹⁵N / δ³⁴S) detect cancer ahead of conventional MCED panels — and at what stage shift?
  4. What does the BGES Hopkins cohort look like at n = 1,000 with full exposure histories, full pharmacogenomic profiles, and full hair isotope panels?
  5. How does the exposure-flux vector feed forward into the digital twin as the E sub-state in CEDT?
