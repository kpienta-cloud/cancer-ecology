Layer 03 · Forecast

The Assimilation Layer.

Tumors are ecosystems with latent interaction matrices. The correct clinical instrument is not a static biomarker panel but a data-assimilating ecological digital twin that continuously re-estimates from sparse, irregular clinical observations.

§ 01

Why static models fail.

Cohort-average models — including the survival models that power most clinical decision tools — describe what the average patient does. They are useful for actuarial purposes and almost useless for steering the specific person in the room.

What the patient in the room needs is a model that takes their data — their imaging volumes, their lab values, their treatment history, their exposome — and produces their updated trajectory each time new data arrive. That is a different mathematical object. It is an assimilating model.

Assimilation is the same trick weather forecasting uses. The model is wrong — every model is wrong — but the model is continuously corrected by observations, and the corrected forecast is dramatically better than either the unconstrained model or the bare observations alone. CEDT brings that trick to oncology.

§ 02

The state-space architecture.

CEDT represents the patient as a structured state vector that evolves through time. Each sub-state is mechanistically meaningful, separately observable, and individually updatable.

State vector X(t) = [ H, E, T, M, Q ] H — host metabolic and immune background; E — exposome flux vector (from ExposoGraph); T — tumor ecosystem composition (clones, niches, edge state); M — therapeutic milieu (drugs, doses, schedules); Q — quality-of-life and functional state.

The factorization matters. Decisions about therapy operate primarily on M; the exposure layer feeds E; the ecosystem-layer G-function shapes T; H bounds what is tolerable; Q is the human floor. None of the five collapses cleanly into the others, so the twin must carry all of them.

§ 03

GLV core, Bayesian assimilation.

The ecological core of the twin is a generalized Lotka–Volterra (GLV) system over the tumor sub-state T: clones interact through a latent interaction matrix, with growth rates conditioned on niches, drugs, and the host environment.

The GLV interaction matrix is not assumed; it is estimated. Each clinical encounter — imaging, lab, biopsy, patient-reported outcome — is treated as a noisy observation of the latent state. A Bayesian particle-filter assimilation step updates the joint posterior over states and parameters. The result is an honest distribution over plausible futures, not a single deterministic line.

This is the layer where the framework finally pays its bill at the bedside: a clinician can ask, on this date, with this drug schedule, what does my patient's trajectory look like — and the twin will return a distribution that is consistent with every observation made of this patient so far.

§ 04

Identifiability and falsifiability.

A digital twin is a scientific object only if it makes predictions that could fail. We commit to three.

  1. Exposure–tumor coupling. In matched-cohort patients, the exposome-derived E sub-state will predict tumor genomic signature distributions ahead of the cohort-average baseline (one-sided, pre-registered effect size).
  2. G-function suppression. Adaptive-dose protocols guided by the CEDT-estimated G-function will produce longer time-to-progression than maximum-tolerated-dose continuation in a head-to-head subset, by a pre-specified margin.
  3. Assimilation gain. CEDT's per-patient forecast error after three update cycles will be lower than a strong cohort-average baseline by a pre-specified margin under proper scoring rules.

If any of these fail, the framework is wrong in a specific, locatable way. That is the point. We did not write the white paper to be unfalsifiable.

Where the data comes from

Cohorts and institutional partners.

A digital twin is a data-hungry instrument. Continuous re-estimation only works if the data pipeline behind it is continuous — in practice, this means embedding the framework inside institutions that already collect longitudinal clinical signal at scale.

Hopkins InHealth. A Johns Hopkins-wide program designed for exactly this kind of work: linking electronic health records, imaging, and biospecimens into a continuously updating clinical observatory. InHealth is the institutional partner that makes CEDT operational rather than theoretical.

The CarcinoGenomics R01 cohort expansion. Scaling the Hopkins BGES cohort to n = 1,000 with full exposure histories, pharmacogenomic profiles, and serial isotope panels is what supplies the longitudinal observation density CEDT requires. The Origin and Forecast layers share this cohort by design.

Forecasting is only as good as the assimilation cadence. Building toward weekly-to-monthly observation density across structured cohorts is the non-glamorous prerequisite to everything else.

Open questions

What we are working on now.

  1. What is the minimum clinical observation density at which CEDT yields a useful posterior — every imaging cycle, every lab draw, or sparser?
  2. How identifiable is the GLV interaction matrix from realistic clinical data, and where do priors do the heavy lifting?
  3. Which sub-state contributes most of the forecast skill in each tumor type — exposome, tumor composition, host, or therapy history?
  4. What is the right user interface for a clinician to consume a probabilistic trajectory at the bedside without misreading it?
  5. How is the twin held to account at the population level — calibration plots, scoring rules, recalibration cadence?
