Layer 02 · Behavior

The Ecosystem Layer.

Once autonomous, the cancer cell becomes a new species and the tumor becomes a town — bounded, habitat-engineering, resource-depleting, edge-managed. Invasion analogies are off by one to two orders of magnitude. Prairie dog towns are not.

§ 01

Why "town," not "invasion."

For thirty years, oncology has spoken in the language of war. Tumors invade. Metastases colonize. Treatment eradicates. The metaphor is so familiar it is invisible — and it describes the wrong biology.

Invasions, in ecology proper, are movements of a species into territory it does not yet occupy, expanding without bound. Tumors do not do this. Tumors are bounded. They occupy a defined territory, engineer that territory, recruit infrastructure, manage their edge, and crash episodically. A prairie dog town does all of these things. So does a tumor. The match is not loose; it is mechanistic.

The price of the wrong metaphor is the wrong therapy. If you believe you are fighting an invader, you reach for total eradication and accept any collateral damage. If you understand you are managing a town, you reach for the levers that control it — resource limits, edge dynamics, predator pressure, and the carrying capacity of the niche. You reach for steering.

§ 02

The G-function.

A G-function — for "fitness generating function" — describes how the success of an individual depends on its own strategy and on the population of strategies surrounding it. It is the right mathematical object for cancer because it captures what no static biomarker panel can: that the fitness of a clone is contingent on every other clone in the tumor, on every drug being used, and on the carrying capacity of the niche.

Fitness generating function (informal) G(u, u*, x) = ∂r(u, u*, x) / ∂u | u = u* where u is a focal strategy, u* is the resident strategy vector, x is the ecological state, and r is per-capita growth. The G-function vanishes at evolutionarily stable strategies — the configurations toward which the system steers itself in the absence of intervention.

The prairie dog town is the homology, not a decoration: the same bounded, niche-engineering, predator-shaped dynamics that explain why a coterie holds its territory explain why a clonal population holds its niche. The G-function is how you write that down.

§ 03

Adaptive therapy.

If you believe the G-function is the right level of description, adaptive therapy is the natural consequence. Instead of escalating to maximum tolerated dose and racing for total kill, you dose to maintain a sensitive sub-population that competitively suppresses the resistant one. You are not eradicating; you are steering.

The clinical demonstration that matters most so far is Zhang et al. 2017 — adaptive dosing of abiraterone in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer roughly doubled time to progression compared to continuous standard dosing, with substantially less drug used. That result is not exotic. It is what the G-function predicts.

The remaining work is to generalize: which tumor types, which drug combinations, which biomarkers signal that the resistant population is being suppressed. The Prairie-Dogs simulator is built to answer those questions.

Open questions

What we are working on now.

  1. Which tumor types have the cleanest sensitive/resistant subpopulation structure for adaptive therapy, and which require multi-drug control?
  2. What is the right observable for "the town is stable" — circulating tumor cells, imaging volumetrics, ctDNA fraction, or some composite?
  3. How do we identify keystone clones — populations whose removal collapses the engineered niche disproportionately?
  4. Can the G-function be estimated, not assumed, from longitudinal patient data — and if so, with how much error?
  5. What is the edge-management signature in human tumors that corresponds to the perimeter behavior of prairie dog towns?

Research programs in this layer

Six threads of behavior we are pulling on.

Endocycling Cancer Cells. Some cancer cells exit the normal mitotic cycle and enter an endocycle — amplifying DNA without dividing, becoming polyploid, sometimes returning. We think these are the keystone cells of the tumor town: rare in number, disproportionate in influence, and a primary source of therapy resistance. Multiple lab members work on the mechanisms (Truskowski, Loycano, Wang, Vitta, Florou, Li).

Disseminated Tumor Cells (DTCs). Solitary cancer cells found at distant sites years before clinical metastasis. DTCs are the bridge between primary disease and lethal recurrence. Understanding what governs their entry, their dormancy, and their reactivation is understanding why curable cancers become incurable.

Cancer Cell Dormancy. Dormancy is not death. It is a reversible state in which a cell waits — sometimes for years — for permissive conditions. The behavior of dormant cells is the central unsolved problem in cancer recurrence and a major focus of the lab.

Tumor Associated Macrophages (TAMs). Macrophages recruited by tumors are not passive bystanders; they become a substantial part of the tumor mass and are co-opted into supporting tumor survival, immune evasion, and metastatic seeding. They are infrastructure of the town, not visitors.

The Cancer Diaspora. A unifying framing: cancer cells leaving the primary tumor are not a wave of invaders but a diaspora — small populations dispersing to new niches, adapting to local conditions, sometimes flourishing, sometimes dormant for decades. The diaspora framing predicts behaviors that the invasion framing does not.

Why Is Cancer Lethal? Metastases themselves rarely cause death directly. Most patients die from the systemic, ecosystem-wide collapse that metastatic cancer induces — cachexia, immune exhaustion, organ dysfunction. The lethality question is its own program because the answer determines what we should be measuring and what we should be treating.
