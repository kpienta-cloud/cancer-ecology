Pillar 03 · Treatment

Treatment of lethal cancer.

How do we treat metastatic, lethal cancer when eradication has failed? Lethal cancer is a bounded ecosystem with latent interaction dynamics. The right therapeutic instrument is not maximal cytoreduction but ecological steering — adaptive therapy, evolution-aware dosing, and a continuously assimilating digital twin.

§ 01

Why “town,” not “invasion.”

For thirty years, oncology has spoken in the language of war. Tumors invade. Metastases colonize. Treatment eradicates. The metaphor is so familiar it is invisible — and it describes the wrong biology.

Invasions, in ecology proper, are movements of a species into territory it does not yet occupy, expanding without bound. Tumors do not do this. Tumors are bounded. They occupy a defined territory, engineer that territory, recruit infrastructure, manage their edge, and crash episodically. A prairie dog town does all of these things. So does a tumor. The match is not loose; it is mechanistic. Where invasion models are off by one to two orders of magnitude in predicting tumor expansion, the bounded-town homology recovers the missing physics.

The price of the wrong metaphor is the wrong therapy. If you believe you are fighting an invader, you reach for total eradication and accept any collateral damage. If you understand you are managing a town — with niches, edge dynamics, resource limits, and internal regulation — you reach for the levers that actually control it. You reach for steering.

§ 02

The G-function.

A G-function — for "fitness generating function" — describes how the success of an individual depends on its own strategy and on the population of strategies surrounding it. It is the right mathematical object for cancer because it captures what no static biomarker panel can: the fitness of a clone is contingent on every other clone in the tumor, on every drug in the system, and on the carrying capacity of the niche.

Fitness generating function (informal) G(u, u*, x) = ∂r(u, u*, x) / ∂u | u = u* where u is a focal strategy, u* is the resident strategy vector, x is the ecological state, and r is per-capita growth. The G-function vanishes at evolutionarily stable strategies — the configurations toward which the system steers itself in the absence of intervention.

The prairie dog town is the homology, not a decoration: the same bounded, niche-engineering, predator-shaped dynamics that explain why a coterie holds its territory explain why a clonal population holds its niche. The G-function is how you write that down. And once you can write it down, you can design therapy that steers it.

§ 03

Adaptive therapy — steering, not eradication.

If you believe the G-function is the right level of description, adaptive therapy is the natural consequence. Instead of escalating to maximum tolerated dose and racing for total kill, you dose to maintain a sensitive sub-population that competitively suppresses the resistant one. You are not eradicating; you are steering.

This is the explicit therapeutic commitment of the Center: steering, not eradication. In a bounded ecosystem with latent interactions, eradication is rarely available and almost always paid for in collateral damage. Steering is. Steering is what the G-function recommends, what adaptive therapy operationalizes, and what the digital twin individualizes.

The clinical demonstration that matters most so far is Zhang et al. 2017 — adaptive dosing of abiraterone in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer roughly doubled time to progression compared to continuous standard dosing, with substantially less drug used. That result is not exotic. It is what the G-function predicts. The remaining work is to generalize: which tumor types, which drug combinations, which observables signal that the resistant population is being suppressed. The Prairie-Dogs simulator is built to answer those questions, and CEDT is built to apply them to the specific patient.

§ 04

CEDT — the state-space architecture.

CEDT represents the patient as a structured state vector that evolves through time. Each sub-state is mechanistically meaningful, separately observable, and individually updatable.

State vector X(t) = [ H, E, T, M, Q ] H — host metabolic and immune background; E — exposome flux vector (from ExposoGraph, Risk pillar); T — tumor ecosystem composition (clones, niches, edge state, dormant DTCs); M — therapeutic milieu (drugs, doses, schedules); Q — quality-of-life and functional state.

The factorization matters. Decisions about therapy operate primarily on M; the Risk pillar feeds E; the G-function shapes T; H bounds what is tolerable; Q is the human floor. None of the five collapses cleanly into the others, so the twin must carry all of them.

§ 05

GLV core, Bayesian assimilation.

The ecological core of the twin is a generalized Lotka–Volterra (GLV) system over the tumor sub-state T: clones interact through a latent interaction matrix, with growth rates conditioned on niches, drugs, and the host environment.

The GLV interaction matrix is not assumed; it is estimated. Each clinical encounter — imaging, lab, biopsy, patient-reported outcome — is treated as a noisy observation of the latent state. A Bayesian particle-filter assimilation step updates the joint posterior over states and parameters. The result is an honest distribution over plausible futures, not a single deterministic line.

This is the layer where the framework finally pays its bill at the bedside: a clinician can ask, on this date, with this drug schedule, what does my patient's trajectory look like — and the twin returns a distribution consistent with every observation made of this patient so far. That distribution is what steering looks like in practice.

§ 06

Endocycling cells — the keystone of recurrence.

One of the defining contributions of the Pienta–Amend lab is the recognition that a small reservoir of endocycling cancer cells drives recurrence in ways that conventional cytotoxic therapy does not address. Endocycling cells exit the normal mitotic cycle and enter an endocycle — amplifying their DNA without dividing, becoming polyploid, sometimes returning to proliferation under permissive conditions. They are rare in number, disproportionate in influence, and a primary source of post-therapy resistance.

We read endocycling cells as the keystone species of the tumor town: pull them out and the engineered niche collapses; ignore them and the town reseeds itself after every round of treatment. Targeting endocycling biology is therefore not an afterthought to cytotoxic therapy; it is its own therapeutic axis, and one of the axes adaptive therapy explicitly tries to control.

This connects directly to the disseminated tumor cell (DTC) and dormancy biology covered in the Detection pillar. Endocycling, dormant, and disseminated states are overlapping descriptions of the same underlying cancer cell behavior: a small population, holding the future of the disease, waiting for conditions to permit return.

§ 07

Clinical translation — where steering reaches a patient.

The Treatment pillar is not theoretical. It reaches patients today through a portfolio of clinical programs at the Brady Urological Institute:

  • Oligometastatic disease and TET trials. When metastatic disease is limited to a few sites, Total Eradication Treatment (TET) trials test whether the entire visible burden can be eliminated and durable control achieved — the curative end of the spectrum.
  • Intermittent androgen deprivation and peripheral androgen blockade for recurrent prostate cancer. The intermittent dosing strategies developed in recurrent disease are the clinical embodiment of adaptive therapy: dose to control, not to maximum.
  • Castrate-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC). The setting where evolutionary thinking matters most. Adaptive therapy with abiraterone (Zhang 2017) lives here. Combination strategies, evolution-aware sequencing, and twin-guided dosing are the next steps.

Each is described in clinical detail on the Patient care & clinic page, with referral information for physicians and patients.

§ 08

Identifiability and falsifiability.

A digital twin is a scientific object only if it makes predictions that could fail. We commit to three.

  1. Exposure–tumor coupling. In matched-cohort patients, the exposome-derived E sub-state will predict tumor genomic signature distributions ahead of the cohort-average baseline (one-sided, pre-registered effect size).
  2. G-function suppression. Adaptive-dose protocols guided by the CEDT-estimated G-function will produce longer time-to-progression than maximum-tolerated-dose continuation in a head-to-head subset, by a pre-specified margin.
  3. Assimilation gain. CEDT's per-patient forecast error after three update cycles will be lower than a strong cohort-average baseline by a pre-specified margin under proper scoring rules.

If any of these fail, the framework is wrong in a specific, locatable way. That is the point. We did not write the white paper to be unfalsifiable.

Open questions

What we are working on now.

  1. Can we treat metastatic cancer as a steerable bounded ecosystem rather than an enemy to eradicate — and double or triple time-to-progression by doing so?
  2. What is the minimum clinical observation density at which CEDT yields a useful posterior — every imaging cycle, every lab draw, or sparser?
  3. How identifiable is the GLV interaction matrix from realistic clinical data, and where do priors do the heavy lifting?
  4. Which tumor types have the cleanest sensitive/resistant subpopulation structure for adaptive therapy, and which require multi-drug control?
  5. How do we identify and target keystone cancer cells — endocycling, dormant, DTC populations — whose removal collapses the engineered niche disproportionately?
  6. What is the right user interface for a clinician to consume a probabilistic trajectory at the bedside without misreading it?
