Overview

The eco-evolutionary framework.

Cancer is an ecosystem inside a human host. Reading it through eco-evolutionary methods is the methodology; Risk, Detection, and Treatment are the three clinical pillars the methodology serves.

The premise

Cancer is an ecosystem.

For most of modern oncology, cancer has been described as an invasion — cells breaching boundaries, colonizing tissues, spreading through the body. Measured against the biology, the invasion model is wrong by one to two orders of magnitude. Real tumors expand at a small fraction of their cells' intrinsic potential. They maintain sharp boundaries, engineer their surroundings, communicate across their edges, and crash episodically under perturbation. These are the properties of an ecosystem — bounded, internally regulated, edge-managed. The Center's working premise is this: cancer is an ecosystem, and reading it through eco-evolutionary methods is the only honest way to predict it, detect it, and intercept it.

Why the methodology matters

An eco-evolutionary methodology gives us tools that single-gene, single-chemical, and static-cohort models cannot. Carcinogenesis is not one chemical hitting one gene; it is combinatorial flux through tissue-specific bioactivation networks. A growing tumor is not a runaway invader; it is a population of clones whose fitness depends on every other clone, on the niche, and on the drugs in the system. A patient's future is not the cohort average; it is a state vector whose trajectory must be assimilated, not assumed. Generalized Lotka–Volterra systems, evolutionary game theory, fitness-generating functions, and Bayesian state-space models are the right instruments. They are eco-evolutionary in origin and clinical in destination.

The three pillars

The visible architecture of the Center is clinical: Risk, Detection, Treatment. Risk asks who gets cancer in the first place — combinatorial exposure on top of genotype, clonal mosaicism, and the host swamp. Detection asks how we catch a new cancer species early enough to change its trajectory — through trace signals in the host environment: hair-keratin isotopes, ctDNA, CTCs, PSMA imaging, dormant disseminated cells. Treatment asks how we steer lethal disease when eradication has failed — adaptive therapy, the G-function, the digital twin. The eco-evolutionary methodology runs through all three.

The unifying commitment

One phrase holds the program together: predict, detect, intercept — at every scale. Predict who is at risk by modeling combinatorial exposure through pharmacogenomic networks. Detect the new cancer species before it establishes, with cheap, non-invasive, population-scale readouts. Intercept and steer lethal disease as a bounded ecosystem rather than try to eradicate it. The rest of the site is what that means in practice.

The pillars

Three pillars of an ecosystem program.

Genes & Exposures

What determines who gets cancer in the first place?

Pillar 02 · Detection

of the new cancer species

How do we catch it early enough to change its trajectory?

Once autonomous, the cancer cell is a new biological species. We detect it the way ecologists detect a new invader — through trace signals in the host: isotopes, ctDNA, CTCs, PSMA imaging, dormant cells.

Pillar 03 · Treatment

of lethal cancer

How do we treat metastatic disease when eradication has failed?

Lethal cancer is a bounded ecosystem with latent interaction dynamics. The right instrument is not maximal cytoreduction but ecological steering — adaptive therapy, evolution-aware dosing, and a continuously assimilating digital twin.
