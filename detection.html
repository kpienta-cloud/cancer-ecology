Pillar 02 · Detection

Detection of the new cancer species.

Once autonomous, the cancer cell is a new biological species — and a different organism demands different instruments. We detect cancer the way ecologists detect a new invader: through trace signals in the host environment.

§ 01

The autonomous cancer cell is a new species.

The scientific anchor of the Detection pillar is the speciation claim. In Pienta et al., 2020 (Molecular Cancer Therapeutics), we argued that the moment a cancer cell becomes autonomous — replicating on its own terms, with its own metabolism, its own selective regime, and its own evolutionary trajectory inside the host — it is no longer a malfunctioning human cell. It is a new biological species. By every operational definition that ecology and evolutionary biology use elsewhere, an autonomous cancer cell satisfies the criteria of speciation.

The implication for detection is concrete. We are not looking for a damaged tissue. We are looking for a new organism inhabiting a host environment. That reframing recommends different instruments. Ecologists detecting a recently arrived invader do not biopsy every leaf in the forest; they look for trace signatures of the invader's presence in the ambient environment — water chemistry, scent compounds, isotopic shifts, hair, scat. Detection of cancer can use the same playbook.

If the cancer cell is a new species, the host carries the trace. Find the trace.

Tools in this pillar

IsoMCED — the hair-keratin isotope screen.

In development Detection

IsoMCED — Isotope Multi-Cancer Early Detection

A 5 cm strand of hair carries a six-month record of host metabolism, structured in keratin. Stable-isotope ratios of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur (δ13C / δ15N / δ34S) track diet, protein turnover, oxidative load, and — we propose — the metabolic signature of an incipient new cancer species.

§ 02

Why isotopes catch a new species in the host.

Early cancers shift host metabolism in characteristic directions: Warburg-style anaplerosis (altered carbon routing), dysregulated nitrogen cycling (altered protein turnover), and disturbed sulfur amino acid metabolism (oxidative pressure). Each of these shifts changes which isotopes get incorporated into newly synthesized keratin and at what ratios. The result is a measurable triplet signature laid down in growing hair long before a tumor produces a ctDNA-detectable signal.

The economics are the second story. At under twenty dollars per sample at population scale, IsoMCED is, if the signature holds, the cheapest population-scale cancer screen ever proposed. It can be collected without a clinical visit, shipped at ambient temperature, and analyzed in batch. That is the kind of instrument a screen needs to be.

§ 03

CTCs, ctDNA, and the liquid biopsy stack.

Circulating tumor cells (CTCs) are intact cancer cells shed into the bloodstream — the literal new species, sampled live. They are rare (one in a billion blood cells in early disease), but enrichment platforms and single-cell sequencing have made them tractable. Our lab has a long history of CTC biology, particularly in metastatic prostate cancer, and we read CTCs as ecological propagules: cells in transit between niches.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is the molecular trace — tumor-derived DNA fragments shed into plasma. Liquid-biopsy platforms read ctDNA for mutations, methylation, and copy number, and can recover signal at very low tumor burden. ctDNA-based multi-cancer early detection (MCED) assays are the closest existing instruments to what IsoMCED proposes; we treat the two as complementary, with isotopes catching the metabolic shift and ctDNA catching the genomic one.

The combined liquid-biopsy stack — CTCs, ctDNA, MCED panels, isotopes — is how we propose to read out the new species in the host without biopsying the host.

§ 04

PSMA imaging — finding the species in the lymph.

For prostate cancer specifically, prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) is an antigen overexpressed on prostate cancer cells. PSMA PET imaging — typically with 18F-labeled tracers — detects cancer foci that conventional CT and MRI miss, especially small lymph-node and distant deposits. It is, in effect, a species-specific tag: light up where the cancer species is, and only where it is.

The clinical implication is large. PSMA-PET changes staging accuracy by orders of magnitude, which changes who is offered curative therapy, who is offered focal treatment, and who needs systemic management. It is the imaging arm of Detection in the disease our clinic sees most often. See Patient care · PSMA Imaging for the clinical-program detail.

§ 05

Disseminated tumor cells and dormancy.

Disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) are solitary cancer cells found at distant sites — bone marrow, lymph node, viscera — often years before clinical metastasis. DTCs are the bridge between a contained primary disease and lethal recurrence. They are the new species establishing a beachhead, sometimes for decades, before it expands. Detecting them is detecting the future of the disease.

Most DTCs do not divide. They sit in dormancy — a reversible state in which a cancer cell waits for permissive conditions in its new niche. Dormancy is not death; it is dormant biology, and dormant biology has its own signals: altered metabolism, distinctive transcriptional states, niche-engineering behaviors. Reading those signals is how we propose to find dormant DTCs before they wake.

The diaspora framing matters here. Cancer cells leaving the primary tumor are not a wave of invaders; they are a diaspora — small populations dispersing to new niches, adapting to local conditions, sometimes flourishing, sometimes waiting. Detecting the diaspora is detecting cancer at the only stage where intercepting it can still be curative.

Open questions

What we are working on now.

  1. Can the isotope triplet (δ13C / δ15N / δ34S) detect cancer ahead of conventional MCED panels — and at what stage shift?
  2. Can a single non-invasive readout — isotope, ctDNA, CTC, or imaging — reliably detect a nascent cancer species early enough to intercept, not just observe?
  3. What is the right combination of liquid-biopsy modalities (isotopes × ctDNA × CTCs) for population-scale early detection, and where do they disagree?
  4. How do we detect a dormant disseminated tumor cell at a distant site — biopsy-free — and predict when it is about to wake?
  5. How does Detection-pillar signal feed forward into the digital twin so that interception is timed rather than just attempted?
